The Lucid Coding Whitepaper

Build with agents. Still know your system.

Agents now write much of the code. The risk is no longer that the code does not get written. The risk is that nobody can say what was built, why, or what proved it works. Lucid Coding is a way of working that keeps that knowledge.

Asif Raja

Version 0.3, draft for comment · 14 November 2026 · The manifesto is the short form of this paper.

Summary

Lucid Coding is the practice of building software with AI agents so that you always know what your agents changed, why they changed it, and what proved it works.

It rests on eight principles and one working loop. People agree what "done" means before the build. One agent builds. A second agent, who never sees the first one's reasoning, checks the real result. Every pass carries proof. Every run stays on the record, and what went wrong becomes a lesson for the next run.

This paper explains the problem, the principles and the reasons behind them, the loop, what a running app should report about itself, how the same practice holds for what a product looks like, how a team can read a system it did not type, what to measure, how to adopt the practice in steps, and what one of our own runs taught us when the critic was right and the bar was wrong.

1. The problem

When a person writes code, understanding comes with the typing. They know why each part is there because they put it there. When an agent writes the code, the work arrives without the understanding.

Vibe coding accepts that trade. You describe what you want, the agent produces something, it looks right, and you move on. That is a fair way to explore an idea. It is a poor way to run a product, for three reasons.

  • The builder grades its own work. An agent that says "done, all tests pass" is reporting on itself. It can be wrong, and it has no reason to look for the case it missed.
  • Looking right is not being right. A screen can look finished while it is slow, makes fifty database queries, or breaks in another language or screen size.
  • The reasons disappear. The conversation that produced the change ends. A week later, nobody can say why the system is the way it is, including the agent.

The cost shows up later, as an incident nobody can explain, or as a codebase the team is afraid to change.

2. What Lucid Coding is

Lucid means clear and aware. In Lucid Coding the agents do the building, and you stay aware of what is being built. Vibe coding is a feeling; Lucid Coding is knowing.

A piece of work is lucid when someone who was not there can answer four questions from the record alone:

  1. What was asked for, in terms that can be checked?
  2. What was changed?
  3. Who judged it, and were they independent of the builder?
  4. What evidence shows it works?

Lucid Coding is not a rule that people must read every line. It is not a ban on fast, exploratory work. It is a standard for work you intend to keep.

3. The eight principles

1. Know your system.

If you cannot say what your agents changed, why, and what proved it, you do not know your system yet. Every other principle serves this one.

In practice
Each unit of work has a written goal, a diff, a verdict and its evidence, kept together.
Sign it is missing
"I think the agent changed that last week" is the best answer anyone has.

2. Give agents instruments, not just screenshots.

A screenshot shows pixels. It hides why they are slow, broken or clipped. An agent that can only look at the screen guesses at causes.

In practice
In development, the app reports its own actions, state, network calls, timings and layout. That instrumentation is removed from production builds, and the build checks that it is gone.
Sign it is missing
The agent fixes a slow page by changing things until the screenshot loads faster.

3. Agree the bar before the build.

Write down what done means, as behaviours someone else can check, before anyone starts. A bar written after the work describes the work. It cannot judge it.

In practice
Each behaviour is a short "given, when, then" statement that names what will be checked and how.
Sign it is missing
The acceptance criteria match the implementation word for word.

4. A blind critic, never the builder, grades the work.

The one who made a thing is the worst judge of it. If the critic reads the builder's reasoning, it inherits the builder's blind spots.

In practice
A separate agent receives the bar and the real result. It does not receive the builder's notes or explanation.
Sign it is missing
Review comments repeat the builder's own summary.

5. Evidence or it didn't happen.

A pass carries its proof: test output, a screenshot, a timing, a log, a link. A claim with nothing attached counts as a fail.

In practice
Evidence is stored with the unit, in a form another person can open.
Sign it is missing
The record says "verified manually".

6. Every run is visible.

What the agent saw, what it built, and why it passed or failed stay on the record, during the run and after it. Anyone on the team can read it without asking the agent.

In practice
A run has a page: its units, their state, the critic's findings and the evidence.
Sign it is missing
The only history is a chat window that has been closed.

7. Every run teaches the next.

Repeated gaps become lessons for the next brief, and past runs set the next budgets. The process improves on the record, where it can be checked.

In practice
After a run, the gaps the critic found are written as short lessons, and the most useful ones are given to the next builder and critic.
Sign it is missing
The critic finds the same kind of gap every week.

8. Get the work done; performance comes with experience and measurement.

Finish the work to the bar first. Then measure what happened: when the page painted, how long each call took, how many tokens it cost. Speed and cost improve from numbers on the record, not from guesses. Context is a cost a run must budget and measure: most of a run's tokens are context read again, not new work. So a run closes a builder when it reports, sends a fix round to a fresh agent with the findings and the diff, gives every agent a context budget, and records its token totals in the retro. In one measured run, 97% of tokens were context read again, builders were 61% of the cost, and fix rounds sent back to the same builder were about half of the builder cost (the figures and the rules).

In practice
Before and after measurements are attached to the unit that claims an improvement.
Sign it is missing
"It feels faster."

4. The working loop

The principles turn into one loop, run once per unit of work.

  1. Intake. Ask every question up front, so the run does not stop halfway to ask.
  2. Bar. Agree the checkable behaviours. A person approves the bar; this is the main human decision.
  3. Build. A builder agent works in its own isolated copy of the code and runs the tests.
  4. Blind critique. A critic agent checks the real result against the bar, without the builder's reasoning.
  5. Evidence. Each behaviour gets a pass with proof, or a fail with the gap described.
  6. Repeat or stop. Fails go back to the builder. The loop ends when the bar is met or the budget of time, money or rounds is spent. Running out of budget is reported as that, not as a pass.
  7. Record and learn. The run stays readable, and its gaps become lessons.

People are in the loop where their judgement matters most: setting the bar, and deciding what to do when the loop cannot meet it.

5. Instruments

An agent can only judge what it can observe. A running app should be able to tell an agent, in development:

QuestionWhat the app reports
What can I do here?Named actions with typed inputs, such as "log in as tester" or "set language".
What is true now?Named snapshots of state, with secrets redacted.
What happened on the network?Calls, statuses and timings.
Why is it slow?Paint and mount timings in the browser; request, query and outbound call timings on the server.
Why does it look wrong?The actual layout: sizes, overflow, clipped text, computed styles.
What did it look like?A snapshot of a named screen, for comparison with the design.

Two rules keep instruments safe. They do nothing until a local, authenticated tool connects. And they are stripped from production builds, with a build step that fails if any trace remains.

6. Visual lucidity: extending deterministic workflows from code to pixels

The principles so far are about what a system does. The same loss of knowledge happens to how it looks, and it is harder to notice, because a wrong colour passes every test. Visual lucidity is the same practice applied to pixels: the look is chosen before the build, written down where agents can read it, measured on the running product, and checked by someone other than its maker.

The drift from words to pixels

An agent asked for something "clean and modern" fills the gap with the average of what it has seen. Each session starts from the conversation it is given, and last week's decisions about the look are not in it. So the look drifts.

  • In apps, raw values appear beside a design system that already has a name for each: a hex colour, a 13-pixel gap, a third typeface, a loading state nobody checked.
  • In games, a new scene gets its own camera angle, lighting and colour grade, figures in front stop standing out from the background, and an effect added for one scene costs a phone its frame rate.
  • In generated images, a character's face, proportions and palette change from one image to the next, and several screens squeezed into one image blur into mush.

The cause is the same in all three. The visual intent was agreed in a conversation and never written into the repository. A conversation is not versioned, the next agent cannot read it, and a critic cannot check work against it.

In practice
Decisions about the look live in files under version control, and change by a reviewed commit, as code does.
Sign it is missing
Two screens built a week apart use different greys for the same thing, and nobody chose either.

Choose the look before you draw it

Improvising taste on a blank page is where agents are weakest. Two habits replace it.

  1. Choose from a few vetted directions. Instead of asking a model to invent a palette, type and layout, offer a small number of complete, proven directions and let a person choose one. Set it with numbers, not adjectives: how dense, how much motion, how much variation from one screen to the next.
  2. One reference image per key view, then measure it. Make or collect one full-size reference image for each key screen, scene or character, never several squeezed into one. Measure those pixels: colours, type sizes and ratios, spacing, radii, camera and light. Write what you measured down. Only then build, from what was written down, not from the prose.

The result is a visual contract kept beside the code. Every entry in it names how it is checked: a computed value, a measured setting, or a comparison with a named reference image.

SurfaceWhat the contract fixesWhat breaks it
App screensNamed tokens for colour, type, spacing, radii and elevation, and which component uses which.A raw value where a token exists, or a new token nobody approved.
GamesThe camera and field of view, the lighting and exposure, the colour grade, and the effects each kind of device can afford.A scene whose camera, light or grade falls outside the stated ranges, or an effect over its budget.
Generated imagesA reference image for each character and style, and a fixed structure for prompts: subject, framing, style, palette and what to avoid.A prompt that skips the structure, or an image made without its reference.
In practice
A unit's bar cites the contract: "uses only the contract's tokens; matches the reference image for this screen".
Sign it is missing
The design system exists only in a design tool the agents cannot open.

Sight that knows what it is looking at

A screenshot alone is a weak judge. It cannot say whether a grey is the token or a near miss. A script that photographs a public page cannot reach a signed-in screen, an empty account, a server error or the third level of a game. Sight becomes reliable when it is paired with control over the state, at both ends, and with the meaning behind the pixels.

  1. Set the state, at both ends. On the server: seeded data that is empty, full, overflowing or failing, with fixed times and ids. In the app or game: this user, this language and text direction, this theme, animation paused, this level and camera.
  2. Capture the region. A snapshot of the named panel or view the change touches, at each screen size the contract names.
  3. Read the meaning. Ask the running product what lies behind that region: the computed styles and the tokens they come from, the fonts actually loaded, the contrast and the margins; or the camera, light and effect values in use.
  4. Compare with the contract. Values are checked exactly. Pictures are compared with their reference within a stated tolerance.
  5. Correct and repeat. A mismatch goes back with the value found and the value required, and the same state is set again to show the fix.

The same state gives the same capture, so a failure can be reproduced and a fix can be shown. An image has no computed styles to read. There the check is the prompt against its structure, and the image beside its reference, judged by a critic that sees both. What reaches a person has already been checked for conformance. The person judges what cannot be measured: whether it is good.

In practice
Each visual check stores the state that was set, the snapshot and the values that were read.
Sign it is missing
The agent fixes a layout by editing styles until one screenshot looks right.

Explore apart, enforce at the gate

Exploring a look and enforcing a look are different jobs. Mixing them is how drift gets merged. The loop keeps them apart.

  • Exploration happens in variant branches, before the build. Builders are asked for a few versions of a screen, scene or image, each in its own isolated copy. Nothing there has to conform. The output is snapshots for a person to choose from, and the choice becomes the contract.
  • A build treats the contract as read-only. A build that changes the contract to pass its own check is caught like a deleted test. A build that needs a new token or reference image stops and asks, because that is a decision about the look, and it belongs to a person.
  • Enforcement is a gate run by the critic. The builder may check its own work against the contract, which helps, but its check is not a verdict. A visual critic, after the code critic and without the builder's explanation, sets the states, runs the check on the commit to be merged, and records the result. A failed check blocks the merge.
In practice
The record shows which variant was chosen, by whom, the contract that followed, and the gate result for the commit that was merged.
Sign it is missing
A design experiment reaches production because it was the branch that happened to be open.

Not all of this exists in tools yet. Setting an app's state, a snapshot of one panel, and its layout and computed styles exist for web and Expo apps. A visual critic after the code critic, a gate result that blocks a pass, and a check that a build did not change the contract exist too. Choosing from vetted directions, measuring reference images into a contract, and instruments for game engines and image pipelines are still being built.

7. Seeing the system you deployed

As agents write more of the code, fewer people will learn a system by reading its source. They will need another way to know what they have deployed. The record of lucid work is that way. It gives four views of the same system.

  • The intent view: the bars and the visual contract. Read together, they are a plain-language description of what the system is meant to do and how it is meant to look, kept current because every change starts by writing a bar, and every change to the look starts with the contract.
  • The change view: the runs. For any part of the system you can see which units touched it, what they changed and why.
  • The proof view: the evidence. For any behaviour you can open what showed it working, and when.
  • The runtime view: the instruments. For any screen or request you can see what it does when it runs, not what the code suggests it does.

Together these let a founder, a new engineer or an auditor understand a deployed product by asking questions of its record. Understanding stops depending on who typed the code, which matters most when nobody did.

8. Adopting it in steps

Nobody needs all of this on the first day. Each step is useful alone.

StepWhat you addWhat you gain
1. Write the barCheckable behaviours before each task.You can tell done from not done.
2. Separate the criticA second agent that sees only the bar and the result.Gaps are found before you find them in production.
3. Require evidenceProof attached to every pass.Claims you can check later.
4. Instrument the appActions, state, timings and layout in development.Critics that judge causes, not pixels.
5. Write the visual contractThe chosen look as tokens, rules and reference images, beside the code.Screens and scenes that stay the same across sessions.
6. Keep the record and the lessonsRun pages, lessons, budgets from past runs.A system the whole team can read, and a process that improves.

For a solo developer, steps 1 to 3 fit in a single session and cost little more than the build itself. For a team, the shared record is the point: reviews, handovers and incident reviews start from it. For an organisation, the same record answers who approved what, and on what evidence.

9. What to measure

These numbers come from the record, so they cost nothing extra to collect.

  • First-pass rate: the share of units that meet the bar on the first critique. Low means the briefs or the bars need work.
  • Rounds per unit: how many build and critique rounds a unit takes.
  • Evidence coverage: the share of passed behaviours with proof attached. The target is all of them.
  • Repeat gaps: how often the critic finds a gap that an existing lesson already covers.
  • Cost and time per accepted unit: counted only for units that met the bar.
  • Escapes: defects found after a unit passed. Each one is a question about the bar, the critic or the instruments.

Whether the loop itself helps can be measured as well. The Coordinator Engineering Benchmark gives a model the same small engineering tasks twice, once through the loop and once on its own, and grades both with tests the model never sees. Its tasks are public. It has no results yet; this paper will cite them when it does.

10. When the critic is right and the bar is wrong

This section tells one of our own runs, because it went wrong in a way the loop should have caught. We used Coordinator to build flight, a new part of reins that records what a deployed server does, checks it against promises written in the repository, and reports when behaviour drifts. We judged the run as a user would: did it get us to the goal? It did not.

What happened

One unit failed five rounds. Every finding was real. Each critic came with a probe on the real code, and each fix came with a test that failed before it. No critic invented a gap or gamed the bar.

But rounds 2 to 5 all sat in the same few hundred lines. Two or more servers share one store. While that store is down, which server sends the notice that says flight cannot see its store, exactly once? The bar asked for one notice. With the store down, the servers have no shared place to agree, so exactly once across them cannot be guaranteed. Every fix moved the hole. Round 1 lost a notice. Round 2 silenced an outage at start-up. Round 3 sent a notice twice. Rounds 4 and 5 each silenced an outage another way.

Each round, the critic described the cause in different words, while the files stayed the same. Coordinator's "same gap twice" check compares those cause words, so it never fired. Its "no progress" check did not fire either: the general critic's scores rose unevenly from 6.5 to 7.8, while the scores of the security critic (a second critic who checks security) rose to 9.2 and fell back to 7.6. That one unit took 36% of everything those runs spent, and the run ended without its goal.

The run collected everything needed to see the loop: the scores, the causes, the files, the cost of each unit. None of it reached the person as a decision. The lead (the agent that runs the loop) also made a design call alone to answer round 2, and that design is where rounds 3 to 5 failed. Fix rounds lost part of their finding lists to a brief that was cut without a warning, and the clock kept running while the person was deciding.

What we learned

  • The critic can be right while the bar is wrong. When fixes keep moving the same hole, check the bar before the code.
  • Same area, different words. Watch the files that keep failing, not the labels on the failures.
  • A bar must be achievable. Exactly once across parties with no shared state is not a bar, it is a wish. Write it per party and state the duplicate: one notice per server while the store is down, one across servers while it is up.
  • Every round should move the goal, not only close the last finding.
  • Ask the value question while there is still budget: would the person who uses this care about this case? Here it was the tool reporting on its own blindness, with several servers, during an outage. It should not have decided the unit.
  • Use what you collect. Evidence that never becomes a decision is cost, not knowledge.
  • Dogfood honestly. When we use our own tools on ourselves, we judge them as a user would: did they get us to the goal? A user would value the critics that caught a write that hung and filled the database pool, and a start that hung forever. A user would walk away from a run that spent a third of its budget on a corner case and needed our internals to explain why.

What Coordinator now does

  • Same files, ask the person. When a unit's last two failing rounds both have serious findings in the same file, the run asks the person, whatever the cause words say: change the bar, simplify the design, accept it as is with follow-ups, or one more round.
  • No progress counts the lowest critic. A round's score is the lowest across the general critic and every specialist (a second critic, such as the security critic), so one critic rising does not hide another standing still.
  • A bar check before the build. Before the bar is frozen (agreed and locked before the build), the lead checks that each behaviour can be met at all. An "exactly once" or "never" across parties that share no state is rewritten per party, with the duplicate stated.
  • A value question at round 3. Before a unit's third round, the lead asks whether the failing behaviour is worth another round to the person who will use the work, and asks the person when the answer is not clearly yes. A finding that needs a design change goes to the person as a question.
  • The clock pauses while the person decides. Time spent waiting for an answer does not count against the time budget, so the round the person approves can still run.
  • No silent brief cut. A brief that is too long is refused, not cut. The findings go in a file the agent reads in full, and a fix round fixes every listed finding.

11. Limits and open questions

  • A critic can be wrong too. Independence removes shared blind spots; it does not make the critic correct. Escapes should be traced back and turned into lessons.
  • A weak bar passes weak work. The practice moves the hard thinking to the start. It does not remove it.
  • It costs more per task. A second agent and stored evidence take time and tokens. The claim is that this is cheaper than the rework and incidents it prevents; each team should check that against its own numbers.
  • Not everything is checkable. Taste, product judgement and ethics stay with people. A visual contract can show that a screen conforms; it cannot show that the screen is good.
  • Open questions. How blind should a critic be on very large changes? How long should lessons live before they are retired? How should records from many repositories be read as one system?

12. Where this is going

Agents will get faster and cheaper, and they will be trusted with larger pieces of work. The amount of software one person is responsible for will grow with them. The scarce skill will be knowing: being able to stand behind a system you directed and did not type.

Teams that keep the bar, the independent judgement, the evidence and the record will be able to move quickly and still explain themselves to users, to colleagues and to regulators. That holds for how a product looks as much as for what it does: a look that is written down, measured and checked by someone other than its maker is one a team can stand behind. That is what Lucid Coding is for.
